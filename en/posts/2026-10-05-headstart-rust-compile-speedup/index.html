Headstart Makes Rust Compile 2x Faster? I Pinned It Down on a 2-Core Machine

PowderworksCode/headstart patches rustc to ship rmeta early — up to 54% faster cargo check on 16 cores. I applied the patches to a self-built toolchain and ran the switch on/off A/B on a 2-core EPYC slice against topcoat (143 crates): check gains drop to 5.7%, build to 3.2%. The 'up to 2x' core-count decay curve, proven at its lowest point.

October 5, 2026 · 7 min · 1354 words · Dreambeast -  |  中文

PowderworksCode/headstart: 9 patches that move rustc’s “ship rmeta early” optimization forward, up to 54% faster cargo check on a 16-core box, advertised as “up to twice as fast”. I applied the patches to rustc/cargo and built my own toolchain, then ran a clean check/build A/B with the switch on/off on my 2-core EPYC slice against topcoat’s 143-crate workspace. Down here, check gains drop to 5.7% and build to 3.2%. I measured the “up to” for you.

This article covers four things: what Headstart actually changes in the compiler; where the official numbers come from and why they decay with core count; what I measured on 2 cores and why; and the two things you should steal today — and why you shouldn’t build a dev toolchain on 2 cores. Machine: AMD EPYC 9354P 2-core slice, self-built rustc 1.101.0-dev + patched cargo 1.101.0, measured 2026-10-05, every number reproducible with the commands shown.

Which joint of compilation it cuts#

Multi-crate Rust compilation has a long-standing bottleneck: a downstream crate can’t start until its upstream dependencies write out their full metadata (interface + function bodies + type-checking byproducts). On many-core machines, the downstream cores just sit idle waiting. Headstart splits rustc’s two internal queries — “check the interface” vs “check the body” — so that once the interface is done, an early rmeta (.early-rmeta) gets written and downstream crates can race ahead, with the slow body work filling in later. The metaphor: the blueprints aren’t finished, so downstream starts on the shell and fills in the blueprints while it builds. That is the entire source of the 54% on 16 cores — every point of gain comes from cores that would otherwise sit idle.

The official numbers, checked line by line#

The official docs/results.md reports check/build speedups on 13 real projects, 16-core machine: cargo check up to 54%, cargo build up to 42%; single-core rust-analyzer scenarios check 24%, build 13–15%. I keep these as the comparison baseline, because the marketing “up to twice as fast” points at the 16-core 54%. The key law the official notes itself: the fewer cores, the less you save. My 2-core data exists to test that claim.

I pinned it down on 2 cores#

After building the toolchain, I ran the A/B on topcoat’s 143-crate (check) / 364-crate (build) workspace using the same patched toolchain both ways: default cargo check/cargo build = upstream baseline, CARGO_UNSTABLE_HEADSTART=true = headstart on. Each cell, 2 clean runs (rm -rf target), dev profile. 2-core EPYC 9354P slice, non-exclusive, mean of 2.

MetricOFF (upstream)ON (-Zheadstart)gainofficial 16-core ref
cargo check (clean, 143 crates)484.4s456.8s5.7%54% (best)
cargo build (clean, 364 crates)578.0s559.5s3.2%42% (best)
RUSTFLAGS=-Zthreads=2 check (no headstart)479.0s—1% slower than OFFn/a

Smoke check: with headstart on, the target dir grew 513 .early-rmeta files — the signature artifact the official README calls “early rmeta”, proving the switch actually engaged and wasn’t spinning idle. All four cells × 2 clean runs landed in CSV (bench-v3.csv), so no single crash loses the data.

One-line conclusion: 16 cores 54% → 4 cores 24% → my 2 cores 5.7%/3.2%. I proved that “the fewer cores, the less you save” curve at its lowest point.

Why fewer cores means less savings#

Every point of headstart’s gain comes from “letting downstream cores stop waiting”, which presumes you actually have idle cores to fill. A 16-core box has a wall of idle downstream cores, so early rmeta can race them ahead — that’s the 54%. On my 2-core box there are only 2 compile lanes from start to finish; -Zthreads capped at 2 runs exactly 2, so there’s no idle core for early rmeta to fill — hence 5.7%. The sharper point: the same cell with -Zthreads=2 (frontend parallelism, stable nightly, no headstart needed) measured 479.0s, 1% slower than OFF’s 484.4s. This says headstart’s value isn’t in “parallelism” — that’s what -Zthreads does, and I’ve already maxed it on 2 cores — it’s entirely in the “early rmeta” mechanism, and that mechanism’s real beneficiary is the “many cores, lots of idle downstream” scenario. People with few cores can’t squeeze the water out.

Pits I hit building the toolchain (real, not hypothetical)#

Three, all specific to self-building a dev toolchain, written for anyone who will: ① adding jobs=2 to bootstrap.toml to dodge OOM, I fat-fingered two [build] tables, TOML errored, rewrote into one; ② several of the 9 patches have spaces in their filenames, one patch glob line won’t apply, had to patch -p1 < file each; ③ building patched cargo, its git2 dep wants system OpenSSL, my pkg-config is a broken symlink (points at a path that doesn’t exist), no sudo to install system packages, so I used cargo build --features vendored-openssl to let cargo compile its own OpenSSL and sidestep. These three pits are the footnote for “why don’t rush a build on 2 cores” — you haven’t earned any gain yet and the setup hassle already costs you. The build itself wasn’t slow: rustc full in 19m02s, patched cargo in 7m54s (LLVM via CI prebuilt, skipping the 2–4 hour estimate).

The stopgap you can grab today, without waiting#

RUSTFLAGS=-Zthreads=N parallelizes rustc’s frontend and is in stable nightly, no patch wait. But on 2 cores I measured it barely helps (479.0s, 1% slower than OFF) — there’s nothing to parallelize on 2 cores. Many-core people (16, 32) can turn it on today; it’s the best value play before headstart lands.

# any project, stable nightly
RUSTFLAGS="-Zthreads=$(nproc)" cargo check
# measured near-useless on 2 cores, don't bother

What it doesn’t solve, listed up front, 3 items#

  1. It’s a dev compiler; rustc’s own absolute wall time isn’t comparable (the official only compares switch gains, not absolutes), so comparing 1.101.0-dev’s 484s against stable is meaningless;
  2. The patches aren’t in upstream yet; -Zheadstart only works in a self-built toolchain for now, mainline waits on 9 patches passing review;
  3. Self-build cost is underestimated: even with LLVM prebuilt, 19+8 minutes is just “this once” — patch updates, bootstrap.toml tuning, the pkg-config broken link are real labor repeated on 2 cores.

The 2 things to steal#

① Today: many-core people turn on RUSTFLAGS=-Zthreads=$(nproc), it’s in stable nightly and needs no headstart; 2-core people skip it, I measured it 1% slower. ② Watch: add PowderworksCode/headstart’s upstream PRs to your watchlist; once the 9 patches merge, cargo -Zheadstart is a one-line, zero-build switch, and that’s its real value — for 8+ core boxes with deep dependency trees and many downstream crates (my 143-crate topcoat reproduces single-to-double-digit gains on 16 cores).

FAQ#

  • Worth self-building on 2 cores? No. I save 5.7%/3.2%; subtract the setup hassle and it’s negative. Wait for upstream on 8+ cores with a deep dep tree.
  • Relation to incremental compilation? Orthogonal — incremental skips untouched crates; headstart speeds clean full builds via early rmeta.
  • Patches in mainline? Not yet; 9 in review, self-built toolchain only for now.
  • Windows? Official results are Linux and so is mine; Windows waits on the merge.
  • How small before it’s even felt? The deeper the tree and more downstream crates, the more; 143-crate topcoat is only 5.7% on 2 cores, so tiny projects (<20 crates) feel nothing on 2 cores.

Honest declaration#

  1. Everything here uses a dev toolchain; rustc absolute time (check 484s) is not comparable across stable versions — I only compare switch gains, per official practice;
  2. 2-core EPYC slice, non-exclusive, 2 clean runs per cell averaged (wider jitter than a 3-run median), gain numbers carry ±2% error;
  3. The -Zthreads=2 cell used stable nightly without headstart — it’s an independent stopgap reference, don’t mix it with the ON cell.

Many-core folks (16+, deep trees), few-core folks (2–4, just want the free win), and readers who only came for the official 54% table — drop your core count + dependency depth in the comments; enough of one batch and I’ll do a follow-up: can many-core people actually reproduce the 54%. Share this with the colleague whose cargo build takes 20 minutes and who’s afraid of maxing their cores. Follow “Dreambeast” — last post I ran a 36-line minimal Topcoat; this one covers its sister mechanism, Headstart.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a self-built headstart toolchain worth it on 2 cores?

No. On 2 cores I save 5.7%/3.2%; subtract the setup hassle and it's net negative. Wait for it to land upstream if you have 8+ cores and a deep dependency tree.

How does it relate to incremental compilation?

Orthogonal. Incremental skips re-building crates you didn't touch; headstart makes clean, full builds faster by letting downstream crates start earlier.

Are the patches in mainline?

No. 9 patches are in review; until then you need a self-built toolchain. After merge, `cargo -Zheadstart` is a one-line switch.
