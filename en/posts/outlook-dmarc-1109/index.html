Outlook.com Is Silently Rejecting Some Mail From Domains Without DMARC: A 30-Second Self-Check

Since 2026-11-05 Microsoft's consumer mail partially rejects domains without DMARC, unannounced. A 30-second dig self-check and the p=none-to-p=reject path.

November 9, 2026 · 9 min · 1748 words · Dream Beast -  |  中文

Since November 5, 2026, Microsoft’s consumer mail service (Outlook.com, Hotmail, Live, MSN) has started refusing part of the mail from domains that don’t publish a DMARC record. No announcement was made: no postmaster notice, no blog post, no service-health entry. If your business emails these consumer addresses, you may be losing deliveries right now and not know it.

This piece is a 30-second self-check for the people who send from their own domain — product people, SaaS, developers shipping transactional and marketing mail. The core is one command: dig +short _dmarc.yourdomain TXT. If it returns nothing, you’re in the rejected batch.

The “Terribly Broken” 5.7.1 Is Usually Just One Missing DMARC Record#

A rejected sender gets a bounce like:

550 5.7.1 Message rejected: Message lacks SPF/DKIM/DMARC Auth Records (AS9031519)

On day one, the same rejection came back without the (AS9031519) code. The wording looks alarming, like the whole sending path is broken. It usually isn’t. The domains that bounced mostly had working SPF, and many had DKIM too; what they were missing was the DMARC record that ties the two to the From address. Plainly: your mail isn’t unsigned — the “I acknowledge this message” paper is missing.

The Part-Rejection Is the Dangerous Part#

Microsoft doesn’t reject all the mail from an affected domain; it rejects some. One message to a Hotmail address lands, the next bounces, a resend may succeed. That inconsistency is the kill point: the sender sees a string of acronyms, assumes a glitch, and scrolls on; the recipient never knows a message was dropped.

For a business that lives on “reach people at personal addresses” — patients, customers, applicants, vendors — this is silent lost delivery. Any address at outlook.com or hotmail.com may have missed a transactional email that should have arrived. And “some of my mail gets through” does not imply “my domain is safe” — partial pass-through is precisely why it’s dangerous.

Quantify it. Suppose consumer addresses are about 40% of your transactional mail and this rejection hits a fraction of those; then of every 10 you send, maybe one or two silently never arrive, while your send log says “sent.” For activation, payment-confirmation, and booking emails, one miss means the user never saw it, opens a ticket, and it gets attributed to “the system is broken.” Lost delivery doesn’t show up in send-side error rates — it hides in receive-side silence.

The Three Most Common Misreads#

“My SPF is fine, so I’m good” — SPF only says “who may send as my domain”; DMARC says “what do I do with what doesn’t align, and how is it penalized.” This rejection is missing the latter; a domain with good SPF still loses delivery.

“I published p=none, that’s enough” — p=none is the floor for passing this line, but it doesn’t reject impostors and doesn’t make receivers trust your domain more. Treating p=none as the destination is a paper you filed without closing the sending boundary.

“I’m low-volume, this won’t hit me” — the published rule targets bulk (5,000/day+), but the new 5.7.1 reaches small senders — a one-line reply to a small-office customer can bounce. Low volume is no longer a talisman.

DMARC Is One TXT Record, Not a System Rewrite#

A lot of developers hear DMARC and assume a new service or an email-architecture change. No. It’s one DNS TXT record at _dmarc.yourdomain, shaped v=DMARC1; p=policy; rua=report-addr. The minimum is v=DMARC1; p=none — p=none is report-only and changes nothing about how other receivers treat your mail; the only effect is that reports start flowing to the rua address.

So the fix for this rejection is one DNS record, live in minutes. It doesn’t change your SPF or DKIM behavior; it just makes the “I have this record” fact visible so Microsoft stops rejecting.

30-Second Self-Check: Run This#

On any networked machine:

dig +short TXT _dmarc.yourdomain

Nothing returned — your domain is in the rejected set right now and consumer-bound mail is dropping. Returned v=DMARC1; p=none — you’ve cleared the floor, safe. p=quarantine or p=reject — stricter than required, safe.

Also glance at SPF and DKIM alignment. dig +short TXT yourdomain and find the v=spf1 record to confirm sending IPs are on the allow-list; DKIM is whatever domain your mail service signs. DMARC’s verdict depends on SPF or DKIM aligning to the From domain, layered with your record.

One more line: if dig returns a record but no v=DMARC1, the record is malformed and counts as not published. Treat the record as a vitals number — not found is unregistered; a clean v=DMARC1 is the line you actually put out.

p=none Is the Floor, p=reject Is the Comfort Zone#

The record this rejection requires you to clear is, at minimum, p=none. But many own-domain senders go straight to p=reject. rexai.top runs v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:postmaster@rexai.top, paired with a v=spf1 mx -all SPF — meaning only that MX may send as the domain; everything else is hard-failed. p=reject is one notch above this rejection’s floor: “I not only declared the record, I told the world that misaligned mail to me is rejected.”

If you’re still on p=none, the safe upgrade path: publish p=none and collect rua reports for a few weeks, confirm the “non-compliant” samples are ones that should be rejected, then step to quarantine, then reject. Along the way you’re continuously receiving reports, so every step is evidence-based, not a blind jump.

The sequence you can do today is short. Run dig +short _dmarc.yourdomain; if it’s absent, add v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:postmaster@yourdomain — live in minutes, stops the bleeding. A week or two later, against the rua reports, confirm your sending IPs are clean, then raise p to quarantine. Two steps done, you’ve cleared this rejection line and started pulling the sending boundary back in.

Why This “Silent Tightening” Wave Keeps Coming#

Microsoft has long had published sender rules: since May 2025, domains sending 5,000+ messages a day to Outlook.com must pass SPF, DKIM, and DMARC or get 550 5.7.515 Access denied. That rule aimed at marketers and bulk; most small businesses rightly assumed it didn’t apply to them.

This is different. It reaches small senders — a one-line reply can bounce. It was not announced. Its bounce only says “records missing” without telling you which link failed. Put the three together and the signal is clear: the consumer receive side keeps raising the sending bar, and it does so silently — no notice, just in force. For an own-domain sender, this isn’t a “should I do DMARC” question; it’s “when do I discover I haven’t.”

In the next wave, Outlook likely won’t be the only one. Once a top receiver makes “must publish DMARC” an actual behavior, other receivers usually follow and the consumer baseline rises together. The record you add today to pass this rejection is tomorrow’s industry default floor. So don’t treat it as one-time firefighting — treat it as the first step of sending-identity governance: publish the record, read the reports, tighten the policy on evidence. What you add today is “does it exist”; what you tighten going forward is “how strict.” Once that line is set, the next receiver that tightens still finds you on the inside of the threshold.

What the rua Reports Tell You — Don’t Let the Acronyms Intimidate You#

After you publish v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:postmaster@yourdomain, receivers start POSTing verdicts to that rua address. Reports are XML with a few core fields: the dmarc node’s domain (the judged domain), policy (the p you declared), disposition (what the receiver actually did: none/quarantine/reject), the spf and dkim nodes each pass or fail, and source-ip.

Read reports with one line in mind: are there any disposition of quarantine/reject where neither spf nor dkim aligns? Yes — those IPs are impersonating your domain to send, find and block them. No — your domain’s sending path is clean and you can safely step up to p=quarantine. Read the report like a physical, not field by field.

What a Passing Domain Looks Like#

Take rexai.top as the sample; it has both records:

$ dig +short TXT _dmarc.rexai.top
"v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:postmaster@rexai.top"

$ dig +short TXT rexai.top
"v=spf1 mx -all"

The _dmarc line declares DMARC, policy pushed to p=reject, reports to postmaster; the root-domain SPF is v=spf1 mx -all, meaning only that MX may send as the domain and -all hard-fails everything else. The two together close the domain’s sending identity: anyone impersonating it gets rejected by p=reject immediately, and its own sending is confined to the legal MX. That’s the “record present, policy strict, boundary closed” state you want after running the self-check.

Honest Boundary#

Microsoft hasn’t announced this tightening; all detail comes from bounces that appeared in multiple send logs on November 5 and public reports, not an official statement. The rejection is “partial” and still moving; AS9031519 is a same-day new code. I verified rexai.top’s dig output, but it has no live sending path, so I did not actually send a message to verify “will it also be rejected.” The self-check above offers the command and the logic; for your domain, run dig and read the rua reports, then the conclusion lands.

FAQ#

My domain has no send business — should I care? As long as your domain can be used as a From (employees sending from it, automated system notices), publish a v=DMARC1; p=none. It changes nothing and just files the missing paper.

Will p=none start rejecting my existing mail? No. p=none is report-only; other receivers’ behavior is unchanged. It just starts collecting rua reports so you can see how receivers judge your domain’s sending.

Is 5.7.1 the same as 5.7.515? No. 5.7.515 is the published rule for 5,000+/day bulk; 5.7.1 is the new, silent code that also hits small senders. One is announced, the other isn’t.

Do I jump straight to p=reject after publishing DMARC? No. Start p=none, collect rua reports, confirm the non-compliant samples should be rejected, then quarantine, then reject. rexai.top running p=reject directly is because it has a clear sending boundary; not every domain should top out immediately.

No announcement — why believe it? Believe the consistent bounce text across multiple independent send logs on November 5 and the new AS9031519 code. Microsoft issued no postmaster notice, but the behavior already happened. Run dig on your domain; a record means safe, none means add p=none first.
