You Spend a Lifetime Preparing for Old Age — But the Number Actually Staring Back Is This One

I saw an old man lying on a Shenzhen street. After I pulled the data I understood it was not an exception, it is a probability: 125 million Chinese households now contain a single person. What decides where you end up is not whether you have children — it is whether there is a second person near you at the end.

October 10, 2026 · 10 min · 1971 words · Dreambeast Coding -  |  中文

On a Shenzhen street I saw an old man lying on the roadside. Not fainted — just lying there, unmoving, having slept in that spot the night before, still there the next day. He was not paralyzed, and he was not ignored on purpose. He was simply ignored. One person, in a city of tens of millions, with no home to go back to.

I did not walk on. The frame stuck: a city that runs on young labor makes no provision for anyone past fifty, and an old man is a cost item on its ledger, not an asset. Passersby filmed him with their phones; a water seller stopped and asked if he wanted a drink, he moved a finger, said nothing. That one question was the only time anyone spoke to him that day.

I kept replaying why I had stopped. It was not pity. It was the clothes — a work jacket worn white, the collar frayed — separated from the polish of everyone else on that commercial street by a line no one can see. He did not belong there, but he could only stay there, because there was nowhere else for him to go. The city gave him the conditions to survive; it did not give him the conditions to survive with dignity.

Then I pulled a set of numbers. Once I had them I understood: what I saw was not an exception. It is a probability. You spend your life preparing for old age, and the preparation is usually one of four moves: get married, have children, save money, buy a flat. But what actually decides whether you die in a bed or on a roadside is a different number — whether there is a second person near you at the end.

One. The number: one in four Chinese households is now a single person#

Start with the total. The Seventh National Census (2020): out of 494 million household registrations, 125 million are single-person households — 25.39%. In one household out of four, only one person remains.

This is not new; it is the acceleration band. From 2010 to 2020 single-person households grew by 67.09 million, more than doubling; the share jumped from 14.5% to 25.4%, a single ten-year step of 10.86 percentage points. The same census shows average household size falling from 4.33 people in 1953 to 2.62 in 2020 — below the “family of three” line.

Regionally, Guangdong has 14.11 million single-person households, the only province past ten million — it is the largest province by resident population and by in-migrants, and the young come in and live alone, not with their parents. Zhejiang’s curve is steeper: single-person households as a share of all households went 13.5% in 2000, 20.4% in 2010, 30.8% in 2020.

Read the two charts together and the trend is plain: households are getting smaller, and getting smaller faster. A 2025 study in the journal Population Research put a colder word on it than any number can — the growth in single-person households has already switched its driver from “young people choosing to” to “old people being left to.” Solo living in the first half of life is a choice; in the second half it is the residue. That is the thing the title’s number is actually watching.

Two. He is not an exception, he is one of the 125 million#

The old man I saw on the street is the direct counterpart of these people.

In Chang’an, Dongguan, a 33-year-old engineer was found dead in his rented room on April 9 by a coworker. Police established he had been dead for close to three days. His payslip showed he had worked 31 consecutive days in March, 190 hours of overtime. The company called it “voluntary extended hours”; he had signed a request.

In Guangzhou, a 32-year-old programmer, Gao Guanghui, collapsed on the morning of Saturday, November 29, 2025 while working from home, and could not be saved. His wife said Monday to Friday he was awake less than two hours a day at home. After he fell, his phone kept ringing; while he was in resuscitation he was being pulled into a work group to chase an order — eight hours after he died, a client was still assigning him work. The case went to court in July 2026; the family sued; no verdict was handed down in court.

Farther away, in Japan. National Police Agency figures: in 2025, 76,941 people living alone died at home with no one watching; 22,222 of them were formally ruled “lonely deaths.” Japan’s single-person household share is 41.4%, far above China’s 25.4% — Japan is this number’s next ten years.

Line these up and what stings is not the misery but the repeated structure: one person in a rented room, one in a dorm, one in his own living room, and after they fall, three days, eight hours, some number of days pass before anyone notices. This is not a question of whether you have children. It is a question of whether the person closest to you is actually in the room.

Three. Why “having someone to rely on” keeps failing#

“Marry and have kids and someone will look after you.” That sentence is failing in four places at once.

The hukou keeps you out of the local safety net. In a city like Shenzhen, an old man without the local hukou lives under a different rulebook for retirement, means-tested aid, and community care. He follows his children across a province, arrives in that city, cannot speak the dialect, has no registered address, and his medical insurance barely reaches across the line — he becomes an island in that city. The benefits of the city he belongs to, he cannot use. The benefits of the city he is in, he is not entitled to.

The city’s growth logic is not designed for old age. Shenzhen is the archetypal “young-labor engine”: you come in at twenty, you get swapped out at forty, and the city pours almost all its resources into making the young willing to come and able to work. Old age is not a KPI here. An old man with no hukou, no portable insurance, and no local ties lying on that road is, on the city’s ledger, not even an object that needs handling.

“Get rich first, then lift the rest” stops at redistribution. The thing you complained about is right: it solves the problem for the people who can earn. An old man in his sixties or seventies with no savings, no local identity, and children somewhere else falls into the place it never reaches. This is not a failure of “growth”; it is that redistribution has never landed on him.

Last, and most direct: the break in community and social services. The first three are “why he has no one to rely on.” This one is “why no one noticed.” A local old man has a record at the neighbourhood office, follow-ups at the community clinic, relatives and neighbours who can knock. An old man with no hukou, children out of town, who just started sleeping on this street, is invisible to the system — no one has ever made a file on him, and no one’s list of responsibilities carries his name. The Dongguan engineer dead for three days, the Guangzhou programmer still being assigned work eight hours after death — neither is “familyless.” The system simply has no grid cell designed for “a fallen person, no hukou, no one claiming him.” The old man you saw that day hit all four at once: no local hukou, out of town, no savings, no one responsible. He was not unlucky; four gaps were stacked on top of each other.

So that old man did not lack children. More likely he had children, but they were not in that city, or could not take him in, or had already stopped being able to look after him. Having children is not the same as having support. What is actually dependable is always four things: money, health, a city you can live in, and the person who will find you at the moment you fall. Children are one of those four variables, not the only one.

Four. What you spend your life preventing was never the number#

Back to you. Not marrying, not having kids, does not mean you will become the man lying on the roadside. You have a craft, diversified income, the ability to work remotely, assets — that is another insurance, harder than the version many peers tied to the system carry. But the thing you should actually be watching is not “whether to settle down.” It is the number itself: when you are old, is there still a second person near you.

Four moves you can make now:

Money. Do not lose the principal. The base colour of old age is a balance sheet, not a family relation. Diversify where you can; do not load up on one city, one asset class, one employer.

Health. Do not wreck the body. None of the cases above died of poverty; they all died of “no one knowing.” And “no one knowing” is, for most of them, because they fell and could not get back up.

Non-kin social ties. A neighbour, a community, an old work group, one person who can knock at 2 a.m. The 22,000 lonely deaths died of “no one noticed,” not of “no one loved.” What you need to solve is being seen, not being loved.

City. Decide which city you will live in when you are old, and get the hukou and medical insurance to move with you. Following your children to a city is worse than following the policy there — the first one changes; the second one you can sort out in advance.

Five. Back to that old man#

You did not walk on that day, and it was not an accident. A city where an old man lies on the road while polished people walk past will hand you that frame again and again — because he is the cheapest part of that city, and the part no one is accountable for.

He did not die of poverty. He died of “no one knowing.” And the opposite of “no one knowing” is not marriage, and not children. It is whether, before you fall, you have put yourself on someone’s list of people they will look for.

The number has been there the whole time. You just do not look at it.

Frequently Asked Questions

If I never marry or have kids, is my old age doomed to be terrible?

No. Old age is decided by four variables — money, health, social ties, and city — and children are only one of them. Having kids does not guarantee support; not having kids does not guarantee abandonment.

What exactly is 'the number'?

Whether there is a second person near you. Macroscopically it is the single-person household share (25.4% in China, 41.4% in Japan); microscopically it is whether someone will know you have fallen within eight hours or three days.

Will solo households keep growing?

Yes. The driver has already shifted to 'passive aging', and falling fertility, a later age of first marriage (24.89 to 28.67), and longer life expectancy are all pushing the share up.

Should 'get rich first, then lift the rest' really stop here?

Growth itself is not the problem. The problem is that redistribution has never reached the people with no local hukou, no portable medical insurance, and children in another city. That is a mechanism gap, not a philosophy problem.

I'm 30. Should I be anxious about this?

Do not sit in anxiety — deploy along the four variables. Anxiety does nothing; being seen does.
