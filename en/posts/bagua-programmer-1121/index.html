How LeetCode Drills and the Front-End/Back-End Split Damaged a Generation of Chinese Programmers

In the age of AI, is drilling interview trivia and carving yourself into a fixed station on the front-end/back-end assembly line still necessary — or already redundant? A clear look at what those drills actually test, what AI changed, and the engineering judgment that still makes a programmer valuable.

November 21, 2026 · 3 min · 585 words · DreamBeast Programming -  |  中文

Opening#

Let me start with a take that might get me roasted: has China’s IT culture of interview drills plus the front-end/back-end split ruined how many programmers?

Don’t fire back yet. By “ruined” I don’t mean it was worthless — my point is that in the age of AI, still using those drills as the ruler to screen people and to prepare for interviews is already a little redundant.

1. It used to have merit#

These drills didn’t come out of nowhere. In the early days, information was opaque and people aligned on the basics quickly by memorizing questions; splitting front end from back end made roles clearer and collaboration smoother. In its day, the system worked — I won’t deny that.

The problem is that it later deformed.

2. What those drills actually test#

They test “specialized techniques”: how a particular API is used, what a framework’s configuration options are, what this snippet prints. In plain terms, they test memory and test-taking, not the full craft of software engineering.

You know the type: they can recite the answers cold and sail through an interview. But put them in charge of taking one requirement from zero to production — how to break it down, which architecture trade-offs to make, how to debug when the site blows up, when to repay technical debt — and they fall apart. Why? Because none of those abilities are covered by a single drill question.

3. The side effect of splitting front and back end#

The same goes for the front-end/back-end split. It began as an engineering technique, but many people turned themselves into a fixed station on an assembly line: front end handles only pages, back end only APIs, and nobody touches the seam in between. Their skills get narrower and narrower. When a problem finally demands crossing that line, they freeze — that territory was never in their “station.”

4. The turning point AI brought#

Then AI arrived, and things changed.

Those drills you memorized? AI spits out the answer in three seconds, more accurately than most candidates can recite. The questions you ground through for months, the model works through in a second.

If you’re still using those drills to interview people and measure a programmer’s level, you’re pitting human weaknesses against machine strengths. If that isn’t redundant, what is?

5. The important “but”#

Let me be fair here: I’m not saying drills are completely useless. You still need the fundamentals. At minimum you must judge whether an AI answer is right and reliable, and hold the line when things go wrong — and that judgment and safety net come precisely from solid basics.

But draw the line clearly: “knowing the fundamentals” and “memorizing drills to pass interviews” are two different things. The first is engineering ability; the second is test-taking craft. What AI is truly eliminating is the latter.

6. Conclusion#

So here’s my view: in the age of AI, a programmer’s worth is not measured by how many drills they can recite. What does it measure? Whether you can take a vague problem and break it down cleanly, work through the trade-offs, and still catch the fallout; whether you can turn an idea into a system that runs, stays maintainable, and actually makes money.

Those are the full skills of software engineering, and they’re what AI can’t replace for you anytime soon.

Stop spending your time memorizing answer keys. Go build real projects, solve real problems. That’s the thing nobody can take away from you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this saying the fundamentals don't matter?

No. You still need them — you have to judge whether an AI answer is sound and hold the line when something breaks. The distinction is between real engineering fundamentals and memorizing trivia to pass interviews.

Was splitting front end from back end a mistake?

It is a legitimate engineering technique. The problem is when people turn themselves into a fixed station on an assembly line: front end touches only pages, back end only APIs, nobody owns the seam, and their skills get narrower until one cross-cutting problem leaves them stuck.

What makes a programmer valuable in the AI era?

Breaking a vague problem down, making the trade-offs, and still being able to catch the fallout — turning an idea into a system that runs, stays maintainable, and makes money. AI can't replace that anytime soon.
