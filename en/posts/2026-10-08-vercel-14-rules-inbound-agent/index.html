Vercel's Inbound Sales Agent: The Prompt Grew From 125 to 1,000 Lines, Then Got Cut to 14 Rules

Vercel COO post-mortem: the inbound sales agent's prompt grew from 125 to 1,000 lines before the team split it into 14 deterministic rules plus 'the model only does judgment' — running a unicorn's entire inbound for roughly $1,000/year. A subtraction methodology for prompt engineering.

October 8, 2026 · 6 min · 1122 words · 梦兽编程 -  |  中文

Prompt bloat is the wall every LLM application builder hits: V1 is disciplined, then every bit of complexity adds a rule, and past a thousand lines the model starts “selectively listening.” Vercel hit that wall, then broke it — COO Jeanne DeWitt Grosser on Tomas Tunguz’s Office Hours: their inbound sales agent’s prompt grew from 125 to 1,000 lines before the team split it into 14 deterministic rules + model-only-judgment, running a unicorn’s entire inbound for about $1,000 a year. This post pulls out every number and quote and distills the transferable subtraction methodology.

The verdict in three lines#

  1. Prompts are grown, not designed: V1 was ~125 lines of qualification rules, written by the team’s best SDR; a year of growth took it to ~1,000 lines, and the model stopped reliably following embedded rules (COO, verbatim: “The model actually won’t always follow some of the rules that are embedded in that prompt.”).
  2. The split point is determinism vs. judgment: rules get engineered as code (a lead arrives → Salesforce lookup for an open opportunity → route to the AE; the model has no say); judgment stays with the model. Inbound now runs on 14 rules.
  3. Cost is a byproduct: once rules are explicit, a second agent polices violations of the 14 rules, decides whether each breach was legitimate, and fixes or blesses it. The whole inbound function runs for ~$1,000/year (self-reported).

The start: one engineer at 20% time + a 125-line prompt#

Vercel stood up a go-to-market engineering team and handed inbound to one engineer at roughly 20% of his time. Version one was a ~125-line prompt written by the team’s best SDR, encoding Vercel’s qualification rules.

The timeline (COO’s own words):

“We kicked it off in June, kept human in the loop from our top SDRs, & by August pulled the human out. So that was sort of what I think succeeded at first, was picking a problem that was reasonably deterministic & then throwing all of our unique context around how do you get the model to behave within Vercel’s four walls.”

Two transferable details:

  • Picking the problem: “reasonably deterministic” — start somewhere concrete enough to verify, not an open domain.
  • The HITL policy (COO, verbatim):

“I almost think about this the way you would think about a human QA. If I’m its manager, I probably read 100% of that person’s first hundred emails while I’m teaching him or her the job. … Then afterwards, I assume that person is now generally competent. And so maybe I do a simple random sample of like one out of every 100 outreaches they do.”

Read 100% of the first ~100; then a random 1/100. Six weeks of that produced enough data, and the inbound agent was live without humans by August.

The bloat: 125 → 1,000 lines, rules start slipping#

Over the year Vercel added product surface and moved upmarket, qualifying a wider variety of companies. The prompt followed to ~1,000 lines and hit this wall (COO, verbatim):

“The model actually won’t always follow some of the rules that are embedded in that prompt. And again, there are a bunch of things in qualification & sales that are actually pretty deterministic. It’s really a rule.”

The diagnosis is calm: a lot of qualification and sales is just deterministic rules; embedding them in a prompt and asking the model to remember is a probabilistic system carrying a deterministic task — the more lines, the lower compliance.

The split: 14 rules + the model only does “where a human would have thought”#

The team cut the 1,000 lines in two:

  • rules: engineered as code. The COO’s example: lead arrives → Salesforce lookup for an open opportunity → if found, route to the account executive. “The model shouldn’t get to decide, we just want that to occur.”
  • judgment: left to the model.

Inbound now runs on 14 rules (COO, verbatim):

“So now actually, our inbound is 14 rules. And we’re really only using the model for thinking exactly where a human would have thought previously.”

That sentence is the thesis: prompts retreat to judgment only; determinism all goes to code.

Two supporting mechanisms:

  1. A breach-monitoring agent: rules being explicit, Vercel runs a second agent watching the 14 — when the system breaks one, it decides whether the breach should have happened, then fixes it or blesses the exception.
  2. Org payoff: every SDR was promoted to outbound, skipping the usual year. COO: “The value of humans is talking to humans.”

Cross-check: 14 isn’t an outlier#

Tunguz ties this to his other piece (Is AI Doing Less & Less?): across 14 production agent workflows, 65% of nodes run as pure code and only 14% stay fully agentic (node share, not Vercel’s 14 rules). Two “14"s coinciding, but the same direction: in production agent systems, deterministic code is the body and the model only shows up at judgment nodes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did a 125-line prompt grow to 1,000?

Business growth: more product surface plus an upmarket enterprise push meant qualifying a wider variety of companies, so rules kept getting appended — up to about 1,000 lines in a year.

What broke at 1,000 lines?

The model didn't reliably follow the rules embedded in the prompt. A lot of qualification/sales logic is actually deterministic, and it shouldn't ride on the model remembering to do it.

How did they split it?

Into rules (deterministic, engineered as code) and judgment (fuzzy, left to the model). Inbound now runs on 14 rules; the model only handles the step where a human would have thought.

What does the whole function cost a year?

Vercel self-reports about $1,000/year (inference + infrastructure) for the entire inbound function of a unicorn. Self-reported; I did not independently verify it.

Can I copy this into my own project?

The methodology transfers: HITL read 100% of the first ~100 items, then sample 1/100; make rules explicit and use a second agent to police breaches and bless exceptions. But Vercel hasn't published the prompt text, so the 14 rules themselves aren't knowable.
