Stop Overpaying for Agents: Local Search, Idempotent Execution, and Thinking Tiers

Three dev.to field reports read together: XERJ local search cuts agent output tokens to a third of grep; one timed-out retry exposes 7 stacked retry layers and a double charge; 7 models on 15 AIME problems show top thinking costs 3.9x with Gemini 4 scores unchanged.

November 17, 2026 · 6 min · 1216 words · Dreambeast -  |  中文

I read three dev.to field reports from last week together and they turn out to be one story: agent money leaks from three different holes. Reading whole files leaks it once, a timed-out retry leaks it again, and a top thinking tier leaks it a third time. Each piece brings its own fix and its own numbers; this post joins them into one checklist.

XERJ: let the agent read passages, not files#

Alex Rashkov’s XERJ External link is a local search engine written in Rust: one binary indexing code, docs, logs, and PDFs, served over an Elasticsearch-compatible API and MCP. The agent stops reading whole files and pulls only the passages it needs, with answers carrying file and line references.

Source: xerj-org/xerj repository OpenGraph header. The author notes this kind of retrieval only helps where the model has not already seen the code.

The project reports two numbers on its own benchmark: output tokens at roughly one third of grep (2.7x) and one twenty-sixth of memory-based answering (26x). Growth is fast: about 19.1k stars, up from 4.2k at the previous write-up, at release candidate v1.0.0-rc.102 (released 2026-11-06).

My reading: the token bill is real — stuffing whole files into context on a private repo is pure waste. But 2.7x and 26x are vendor-reported with no independent reproduction, so cite them with that attached. The rollout I would run: a small side-by-side on the same task set, full-context on one arm and XERJ passages on the other, logging output tokens and solve rate per arm before switching.

A timeout is not a failure: give tool calls idempotency#

Rudratosh Shastri’s piece is about the most expensive leak: an agent calls a payment tool, the first call actually succeeds, the response is lost, the agent sees a timeout and calls again — the customer is charged twice. The example invoice is INV-2048 for $480.

Source: figure from Shastri’s original post — first charge succeeds, the response is lost, the second call double-charges.

The key sentence is precise: a timeout tells you what the caller observed, not what the server committed. The server’s view can be success while the caller’s view is unknown, and treating unknown as failure is exactly how duplicate side effects happen.

Agents make it worse: an execution chain has about 7 layers that can each retry — the model, the agent framework, the tool executor, the HTTP client, the queue, the worker, and the payment SDK. Each layer thinks it is helping; together they double-charge.

The fix is a combination, and its core is splitting two IDs apart:

  • tool_call_id (e.g. call_A123, call_B456) identifies one invocation and changes on retry;
  • operation_id (e.g. op_9f71c3) identifies the user’s one real intent and must be reused across retries.

A fresh call ID must never automatically mean a fresh payment. The server uses the operation ID as the idempotency key (Idempotency-Key: op_9f71c3) and passes the same stable key downstream.

The post gives the full engineering set: a Postgres operation ledger (tool_operations, primary key tenant + operation_id), a 7-state machine (PENDING / IN_PROGRESS / SUCCEEDED / UNKNOWN / RECONCILING / RETRYABLE / FAILED_FINAL), a request fingerprint SHA256(tenant + invoice + amount + currency + type) against same-key-different-parameter reuse, the transactional outbox for the dual-write problem, and exponential backoff with jitter. It cites the idempotency definition in RFC 9110 Section 9.2.2, AWS retry-safety guidance, the IETF Idempotency-Key draft-08, and the documented at-least-once behavior of SQS and Lambda event sources.

The two rules to copy home: record every timeout as UNKNOWN and reconcile, never as failure; and return honest results to the agent — status: UNKNOWN / retry_allowed: false instead of “failed” — or the model will promptly invent a brand-new operation. The post also lists 10 fault-injection tests, the first being “provider succeeds, response dropped”, with exactly one side effect allowed after reconciliation.

Top thinking is not top score: price the tier before you pick it#

xbill’s piece is a hardcore money-burning benchmark: 7 models (Gemma 4 26B/31B, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, 3.8 Flash, Gemini 4 Hydrogen/Carbon/Argon) on the 15 problems of AIME 2025 paper I, each model run twice — lowest thinking setting and highest — with a uniform 32,768-token output cap, a hard budget, and full spend logging.

Source: cover from the xbill/gemma4-dev benchmark. 7 models, 15 problems, two tiers, $7.47 total spend.

The verdict is blunt: both arms together cost $7.47 ($1.53 low, $5.95 high, 3.9x) and the expensive arm bought almost no score. Hydrogen, Carbon, and Argon solved all 15 at both tiers; the smaller models each gained one or two problems, but on 15 problems a gain needs about 35 points to rule out luck, the largest gain here is 13 points (2 problems), and the paired-test p-values run 0.5 to 1 — all plausibly noise.

The cost-side numbers are worth copying out one by one: the top tier cost 2.61–4.75x the bottom on every paid model (Flash-Lite 2.61x, Argon 3.24x, Carbon 3.93x, 3.8 Flash 4.20x, Hydrogen 4.75x); Argon thought 215 tokens per problem on low and 4,129 on high, Hydrogen from 2,468 to 15,062; wall time stretched 3.2–5.9x. Cheapest correct answer is Argon on low at $0.0162; the same 15/15 on Hydrogen high costs $0.1232 and Carbon high $0.1297 — 7 to 8 times more. Two real-world footnotes: Carbon and Argon output prices double from $10 to $20 per million on 2026-12-01; Gemma 26B on the free tier timed out twice and hit the output cap 7 times.

The author’s playbook is directly reusable: start Gemini 4 on the lowest tier for hard math; for smaller models, measure on your own problems before paying 2.6–4.2x. One commenter recomputed the tables from the evidence folder and confirmed the cost tables, Wilson intervals, and McNemar p-values — with one correction: the “4,129 vs 11,613–15,742” range mixes a Gemma number into a paid-model ceiling, so read the per-model table in Step 6 instead.

Three stories, one ledger: keep books on your agent#

Joined together, the action list is three lines: pull passages on the read side (the XERJ route, with a small token-and-accuracy side-by-side first); deduplicate by operation_id on the write side (ledger + fingerprint + UNKNOWN reconciliation, never call a timeout a failure); start on the low tier on the thinking side (Gemini 4 already scores full marks on low for hard math, and re-budget at December prices). Recompute each claim on a small sample first: the 2.7x, the 10 injection tests, and the 3.9x all ship with reproduction paths in their originals.

Honest scope: the XERJ token multiples are vendor-reported benchmarks I did not run; the idempotency piece is an engineering design, not a measurement, with no independent verification; the thinking-tier numbers come from the author’s published replies, logs, and derived tables, with a third party already re-verifying the cost tables. The $0.0162 Argon figure uses pre-increase output prices and doubles after 12-01.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can XERJ replace grep or a vector store outright?

It depends. The pitch is one local binary, file-and-line references, and the agent pulling only the passages it needs; reported output tokens are 2.7x below grep and 26x below pure-memory answering. Those are vendor-reported benchmarks with no independent reproduction — run a small side-by-side on your own private repo first.

What is the minimum viable idempotency setup for an agent calling a payment API?

Three pieces: a server-generated operation_id reused across retries; a request fingerprint (tenant + invoice + amount + currency + operation type) to reject same-key-different-parameter reuse; and timeouts recorded as UNKNOWN with reconciliation, never as failure. Pass the same stable key downstream where supported, otherwise look the order up by merchant reference.

Which thinking tier should Gemini 4 use on hard math?

In this 15-problem run all three Gemini 4 models scored 15/15 on the lowest tier, so start there — Argon on low cost $0.0162 per correct problem. Note Carbon and Argon output prices double on 2026-12-01, so re-run the budget at the new prices.
