Don't Let the AI Write Your Code: A Haskell Developer's Anti-Burnout Playbook

A 10,000-word, 100% human-written post on the Haskell Discourse blew up on HN (163 points, 216 comments). A professional Haskell developer draws a precise line through LLM-assisted development: the machine plans, researches, and reviews — you write the code. Here's the reasoning.

September 27, 2026 · 5 min · 902 words · DreamBeast -  |  中文

A 10,000-word post on the Haskell Discourse is one of the most-discussed threads on Hacker News this week — 163 points, 216 comments, and the comments aren’t about the technical details. They’re about how programmers feel.

The author, turion, is a professional Haskell developer. He heads off the obvious objection first: “Don’t mistake me for a pro-LLM techbro. This is 100% human-written.” The three anxieties he actually addresses are very concrete: AI burnout, the fear of being undercut by “someone with little programming skill and a huge Claude account,” and watching code quality in your own projects decay.

His answer is neither radical nor surrendering: give the machine everything except the coding.

The one-line version#

Let it plan, let it research, let it review, let it do the tedious refactors — you keep the keyboard. The exact line: “Make the agents group around your way to work, not the other way around.”

The value isn’t anti-AI posturing. It’s that every boundary he draws is specific enough to copy into your own workflow today.

Where he draws the line#

1. “You are the coder” is the pivot. The default agent harness runs “plan first, then let the agent code.” He refuses that half: plan together, then you code. The agent surveys the codebase, lists the todos, flags every site to touch and the pitfalls — you start with a fully-fed todo list. In his words, the work got “more fun, sometimes more than before LLMs,” like agile development with all the annoying process stripped out.

2. Codebase ownership. Fully generated code becomes an “LLM wasteland” that only agents can thrive in. And skills atrophy: a few weeks of handing everything over, and picking the keyboard back up feels hard. His claim: LLMs are overrated at good, human-readable code. They’re fine continuing to work inside their own output — you are not, when you have to read and verify it.

3. The counterintuitive use of research mode. The tempting move when an agent is researching is to make coffee and watch it “think.” Don’t: research the same topic yourself in parallel. “The point of making an agent research is not for it to present all the relevant knowledge… The point is that you don’t have to go ’let me google that for you’ on it.” Have it write findings down with source links. When it later proposes something weird, ask which source said that — 50% of the time it finds its own mistake.

4. The GAN-style review cycle. One model generates, one discriminates; together they beat the generator alone. Applied here: do not read any LLM artefact — including plans — without an automated review cycle passing over it first. An agent’s job isn’t done when it hands over; it’s done when a reviewer agent has nothing left to find. He runs the cycle over his own code too and says it routinely surfaces real bugs.

5. Don’t bet on frontier models. Four reasons; the most practical: the fanciest models burn the most tokens, so you can’t guarantee finishing a task in one session — “everything you can do with a smaller model is a safer bet.” The bonus: a workflow that doesn’t need frontier models can eventually run on open weights, off the “technofeudal lords” entirely.

Two lines worth bookmarking#

Stop treating token depletion as “I bought too few.” It’s a service outage. Your workflow runs on a quota someone else can change without telling you. Have offline work ready, the way you’d cache assets for a train trip.

LLM text isn’t human text — especially when the model doesn’t actually know what it’s talking about. Treat gibberish as potentially harmful to your mental health. Talk to people about the code; read machine output only after the review agents have taken the edges off.

The top HN comment (enobayram) seconded the last point: reading mountains of LLM gibberish is “bad for my brain at a physiological level… The effects linger.” His phrase stuck: “the brain’s asbestos.”

Honest notes#

This is a translation-level retelling, not a benchmark — the source is an opinion piece and there’s nothing to reproduce. Source stats at capture time (Sept 27): 19,000 views, 60 likes on the forum; HN 163 points / 216 comments. What I agree with is the engineering discipline in the boundary-drawing; whether “you write every line” is the right call depends on your team’s tolerance — pick a side below.

Which camp are you in?

  • Camp A (hands-only): LLM for autocomplete and docs, zero generated code. The sourcehut/codeberg “no-LLM FLOSS” line is getting clearer every week.
  • Camp B (the author’s): AI for planning, research, and review; human for the code. Self-reported ~2x faster — conspicuously not “10x vibe coder.”
  • Camp C (full agent): spec in, agent codes, you only accept. A commenter’s pushback: “how could I enjoy programming without LLMs now that they take the grunt work off my plate?”

Drop your camp and your real speedup in the comments. Enough data points and this gets a follow-up comparing the three camps side by side.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the core claim in one sentence?

Give the LLM everything except the coding: planning, research, automated review, tedious refactors — you write the code yourself. In the author's words: make the agents group around your way of working, not the other way around.

Why does he oppose full-vibe-coding?

Not on efficiency grounds — on ownership and maintainability: a fully generated codebase becomes an LLM wasteland that only agents can navigate, and your own skills atrophy within weeks. He argues LLMs are overrated at producing *good, human-readable* code.

What exactly should the LLM do?

Treat it as a natural-language bookkeeping tool: turn expert conversations into todos, organize test results, survey the codebase and list every place to touch plus the pitfalls, run adversarial-style review cycles. The discipline: never let it make crucial decisions — make it ask you.

When you run out of tokens, who's at fault?

His reframe: stop treating token depletion as 'I bought too few' and start treating it as a service outage. Your workflow sits on a quota someone else can change without notice, so always have offline-capable work queued.

Is the original post AI-generated?

The author states it up front: 100% human-written, no AI assistance — 'since people have mistaken my texts for LLM-generated before.' 19k views, 60 likes on the forum.
