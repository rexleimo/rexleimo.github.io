50,000 Fake Stats Went Live, Google Forgot Them, ChatGPT Kept Citing Them — 7 Months Later I Read the Receipt

A public experiment from peec.ai: a brand-new domain generated 50,000 invented statistics with GPT-4.1 in a single day, for roughly $50 in total. Seven months on, Google deindexed it but ChatGPT never stopped pulling from it — 330+ referring domains, and the fake numbers ended up in an academic paper and a bank's investment research. I tear the citation-laundering chain apart and explain why sourcing every number is the one control that actually stops it.

October 21, 2026 · 7 min · 1459 words · Dreambeast -  |  中文

The thing I am most stubborn about is that every number I publish must trace back to a source. So when I saw Metehan Yesilyurt’s write-up of this experiment on peec.ai (published October 7), I stopped and read the whole thing — because the conclusion lands directly on the habit I keep. I break the experiment down below, then explain why I think it is more than a content-marketing stunt.

How crude the setup actually was#

In March, Metehan bought a brand-new domain, stateglobe.com, and used GPT-4.1 to generate 50,000 statistics in a single day, covering SEO, content marketing, social, e-commerce, and web tech across 200 countries. Every one was invented; he never checked a single number. The site went up on the cheapest AI web-design template, about $30. Then he added 15,000 listicles on top, about $20 more. The whole ledger: 50,000 fake data points for roughly $50 total. I did not laugh when I read that, because it is crude in exactly the right place — crude enough to show the thing works without any quality of content behind it.

What seven months in#

On Google’s side: the first week nearly the whole site was indexed through indexer tooling and it started showing up in results. Then two Google updates slammed it down, and for months afterward it is essentially invisible, with indexed pages bouncing between 5 and 20 impressions. He says he even tried a subdomain to lift impressions — it did not help.

On the ChatGPT side: it never stopped. At month seven, Cloudflare’s crawl data shows 600,000+ bot requests in the last 30 days alone, of which roughly 72,000 came from ChatGPT-User — the agent ChatGPT uses to open a page in real time while answering someone. So these are not training crawls; each one is a real user asking a relevant question, ChatGPT opening a fake-stats page, and writing the invented number into the answer. GA4 shows 10,000+ sessions coming directly from ChatGPT. By Ahrefs and Semrush, the site has 330+ referring domains, including Shopify, DHL, and Bluehost. On October 7, peec’s own visibility dashboard ranked stateglobe second on a set of tracked prompts, behind only Statista.

My reaction: the Google half is expected — the anti-spam machinery deindexes a site of 50,000 fake numbers. The second half is where it gets counterintuitive: a site that is dead on Google is alive and well inside LLM retrieval. The author’s phrase is “retrieval is not ranking,” and that distinction is the most valuable sentence in the piece, so I unpack it.

Why LLMs eat fake numbers: four mechanisms, all spelled out#

The author lays out the exact pipeline of how an LLM hunts for a number to fill a gap, and I check each one against what I feel myself:

Fanout queries ask for statistics on purpose. When an LLM writes something that needs “a check,” it splits the prompt into sub-queries and adds modifiers like “statistics,” “data,” “report,” or the current year. A page built around “topic statistics 2026” therefore sits near the top of what the model actually searches for — it optimizes for how models search, not how humans type.

A number looks like evidence. “Remote work is growing” carries little weight; “X% of employees work remotely at least once a week” is specific, quotable, and immediately copy-pasteable into the next article. In the model’s pipeline, a concrete number is the highest-ROI material for filling a knowledge gap — and the experiment shows it does not care whether the number is true, only whether it looks true.

The model does not verify the number. This is the part that made me sit up. Search-grounded LLM generation has a latency and cost budget: fetch a few pages, pull the relevant passages, start writing. Verifying a single statistic would mean finding independent sources, cross-checking, and resolving conflicts — across billions of prompts that becomes too slow and too expensive to run on every one. So the system runs a reranker, and the reranker scores relevance, not truth. If a passage matches the query and looks authoritative, it goes into the answer.

Statistics carry a built-in freshness. A stats page has the year in its title, its headings, and its URL — three signals at once telling the model the page is fresh, and recency bias feeds right into that.

The part that genuinely got me: citation laundering#

The dirtiest loop in the experiment is this: a fake number on day one, on a brand-new domain, has zero authority. Then an academic paper cites it. Then a bank’s investment research cites it. From that point on, the next person — or the next model — that finds the number sees a statistic with a source, and that source is cited by serious institutions.

The number never became true. What got more trustworthy with every layer of citation was its appearance. The worse direction the author himself points at: these repeatedly-retrieved pages flow into web crawls, and web crawls flow into training corpora — a number that started as a GPT-4.1 guess can become something the next model “knows.” His exact words: “I hope it hasn’t!” I read that exclamation more than once.

Against my own practice: I have published batches of measured benchmarks where every number carries its original source, and a few readers have asked why I bother. This piece is the answer — not because every number will be taken as true, but because in the LLM pipeline numbers get taken as true at essentially zero cost. Sourcing is not a personal quirk of mine; it is the one control that intercepts citation laundering in this chain.

The control experiment he ran with real data#

After joining peec, Metehan ran the test again at peec.ai/ai-search-geo-statistics — real data this time, every stat verified and properly sourced — and almost immediately started receiving links from sites he suspects are AI-written. His read: the fake version earning links proves that “publishing statistics” as a format works; the real version proves the same format, pointed at verifiable data, works just as well, only clean. Two runs, one conclusion: a statistics page is the strongest carrier for LLM citation — dirty data wins once, clean data wins for the long run.

If I were to build a stats page that gets cited by LLMs#

The author ships a checklist; I run it against my own scenarios and keep the four I think matter most:

One claim per sentence. Retrieval works on passages, not pages. A number that only makes sense with the paragraph above it will probably never make it into the model’s citation pool.

Source and year next to every number. “According to [Source] (2026), X%…” helps the model, helps the human editor, and helps your own credibility. This is the highest-return piece of information hygiene there is.

Headings should match the fanout queries. Not the keywords a human types, but “topic statistics,” “topic market size” — the queries a model actually runs.

Do not hide the data in JavaScript. Server-rendered HTML; check your robots.txt and WAF rules so you are not silently blocking AI retrieval bots.

On the measurement side he lists four I am keeping verbatim: AI-retrieval-bot activity in Cloudflare (ChatGPT-User / OAI-SearchBot / PerplexityBot, not just training crawlers), referral sessions in GA4 from chatgpt.com / perplexity.ai, citation tracking across a fixed set of prompts, and new referring domains in Ahrefs/Semrush. The first two need no tooling at all — that is what I want to do next week.

The one thing I am not going to copy#

The ethics section at the end, I largely agree with, with one question I am keeping open: by the time “never publish fake content” is the rule, he had already put 50,000 fake stats on the internet for over a quarter, and they had by then ended up in a paper, a bank’s research note, an Indian economics publication, and a Canadian report. He now carries a disclaimer and keeps the site up for research, and I understand that — this is a public, reproducible experiment, not a malicious campaign. But the fact that fake numbers already landed in downstream content is not undone by a disclaimer. Where that line sits, the industry has not worked out yet, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

My standing practice: every number I ship carries its original source, and the comment section does not accept “the number looks right.” Have you seen a number that LLMs keep citing that later turned out to have a shaky or outright invented origin? How do you decide whether to keep using it, or to steer clear of that class of numbers altogether — walk me through your handling path.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this experiment real or just vendor marketing?

It is a public experiment by a researcher at peec.ai (an AI-search monitoring company), on the domain stateglobe.com. He states it plainly: this is research, not a playbook for fake content, and the site still carries a disclaimer. Vendor motive exists, but the experiment is publicly verifiable.

Did the fake numbers become true?

No. The numbers never became true. What changed is the veneer of credibility — each citation makes them look more like sourced data. That is the core of citation laundering.

What is the actual difference between Google and LLMs here?

The author's phrase is 'retrieval is not ranking': Google ranks, and its spam machinery deindexes a 50,000-fake-stat site. LLM retrieval pulls data on demand — a site that is dead on Google can still be very alive in an LLM's retrieval pipeline. The two channels have to be tracked separately.

What should I do about the data I publish?

One claim per sentence, source plus year next to every number, headings that match the fanout queries models actually run, and server-rendered HTML instead of data hiding in JavaScript. Dirty data wins once; clean data wins for the long run.

Why does this matter to my own measured benchmarks?

It turns sourcing every number from a personal habit into the one control that actually stops fake numbers from laundering their way into looking trustworthy. In the LLM pipeline, numbers get treated as true at essentially zero cost — the source link is the intercept point.
